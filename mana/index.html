    Mana

    Mana is life.

    It's in a newborn's first breath, in the slow pulse under an old man's skin, in tree roots buried under winter soil. Most people never feel it, never see it, never think twice about the mana that kept their heart beating.

    Maybe one in twenty finds out, usually somewhere around adulthood, that each breath leaves something behind. In them it gathers instead of passing through, at first it's barely something, warmth under the skin, strength that comes too easily, a moment where the world seems to slow down for no reason. Then, if they're lucky, someone teaches them what it means.

    Training lets the body hold more, move it faster, waste less on the same motion. Talent helps, discipline helps more. There are supposed limits to all of this, though enough people have broken them over the years that nobody seriously calls a limit absolute anymore.

    Past all of that are the ones who make ordinary strength look like a joke. Warriors who lift what should take a team of oxen. Men who can hear a whisper from across a crowded courtyard. Swordsmen who cross a room and leave ten bodies on the floor before the first one lands. The strongest have torn through stone and timber bare-handed. There are old accounts of single warrior leaving behind enough wreckage that people assumed an army had come through.

    That kind of power doesn't go unnoticed, neither does the mana itself. Spend it and it leaves traces of an invisible residue that lingers where violence happened, carrying something of whoever left it there. No two are quite the same, like a fingerprint pressed into the world.

    For most people, mana is just the line between being alive and being dead.

    For a few, it's the line between what they were born with and what they could become.

    Mana Reinforcement and Resistance

    As a Praedari becomes more experienced, mana does more than provide power for deliberate techniques. It begins to settle deeper into the body itself.

    A properly trained Praedari develops a natural resistance to attacks carrying no magical property.

    Mana reinforces flesh, bone, organs, and the structures beneath them. Steel still cuts. Bullets still strike. Fire still burns. But considerably more force is required to produce the same injury that an ordinary person would suffer.

    A blade that should cut deeply may leave only a shallow wound. A bullet may fail to penetrate properly. An impact capable of breaking an ordinary man's bones may leave a trained Praedari bruised rather than crippled.

    They are not invulnerable. Sufficient mundane force can still overwhelm this reinforcement.

    That begins to change among Exceptional Praedari.

    At that level, mana is no longer merely strengthening the body.

    The concentration of mana within them becomes so great that it develops a kind of density of its own, permeating the body and resisting anything attempting to pass through it. Flesh remains flesh, but reaching that flesh becomes another matter entirely.

    Against such a Praedari, an ordinary weapon may strike perfectly and still fail to produce a meaningful wound.

    Bullets flatten or stop. Blades fail to bite deeply enough. Shrapnel, arrows, and ordinary weapons may still throw the body, tear clothing, or leave superficial marks, but they cannot reliably overcome the density of mana protecting what lies beneath.

    For an attack to properly wound an Exceptional Praedari, a magical property is mandatory.

    The attack must carry mana of its own.

    Even then, merely possessing magic does not guarantee a serious injury. The mana behind the attack must possess enough power to contest the mana protecting the target. Weak enchantments may penetrate where mundane weapons cannot, yet still accomplish little against someone whose mana vastly exceeds their own.

    The greater the Praedari, the more severe this difference becomes.

    To reliably injure them, the attacking force must increasingly approach their own level of mana.

    Against the strongest Praedari, this creates a brutal reality:

    power must be answered with power.

    An ordinary soldier may fire directly into one and accomplish almost nothing. Give that same soldier a properly enchanted weapon and the attack can finally draw blood. Give him a weapon carrying mana strong enough to rival the target, and the wound becomes something the Praedari can no longer simply disregard.

    This is why magical weapons become a necessity rather than a luxury when fighting the highest ranks of Praedari.

    Against ordinary people, enchantment makes a weapon better.

    Against an Exceptional Praedari, enchantment is what makes the weapon a weapon at all.
